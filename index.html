How a case should feel

It can be a conversation between two people, or simply an easy-to-follow story. The goal is understanding, not performance.

Look for the pattern

One failure is a story. Many failures start showing us patterns we can recognize elsewhere.

MarketDid enough people actually want this?
MoneyDid each sale help the business or hurt it?
Cash flowCould the business survive the gap between paying bills and getting paid?
PeopleWere the founders, leaders and key hires working well together?
OperationsCould the business actually deliver what it promised?
StrategyDid the company grow or change direction before the basics were working?
The bottom line

If you were starting this business tomorrow...

Every case ends here. Not with a vague lesson, but with things you could actually check before putting your time or money into a similar business.

☐ Who is the real paying customer?
☐ Why will they keep buying?
☐ What do I actually make from one sale?
☐ What happens if sales are much lower than expected?
☐ Where could cash get stuck?
☐ What warning sign would make me stop?

Simple to read. Serious underneath.

The writing is friendly, but the research is not casual. We separate what we know from what we think.

  • Verify important facts from credible sources.
  • Build the timeline before explaining the failure.
  • Separate the final symptom from the deeper cause.
  • Look for warning signs that were genuinely visible at the time.
  • Do not turn hindsight into certainty.
  • Clearly label uncertainty when the evidence is incomplete.
One rule we protect.

We won't write a founder's story as if we already knew the ending. A failure page should help the reader understand what someone could reasonably have known and checked at the time.
