Case #001 · India · Delivery / Quick Commerce

Dunzo

How a useful local-delivery business moved into a much harder game — and what we can learn before making a similar move.

Started2014
Major 2022 raise$200M from Reliance
Total raised~$450M reported
Later statusCIRP began Aug 2025

First, what actually happened?

Question

Was Dunzo a bad business from day one?

Simple answer

No. That would be too easy — and probably wrong.

Let's unpack it

Dunzo began as a local delivery and "get this for me" service. People knew what it was useful for.

Then?

It moved much harder into quick commerce: groceries, dark stores and very fast delivery.

That put Dunzo into a fight with companies that were also spending heavily and expanding quickly. Public reporting describes a combination of aggressive expansion, high cash burn, difficult unit economics, operational problems, fierce competition and a later funding crunch — not one magic mistake.

Who loses when a company closes?

Investors

Shareholders can lose some or all of the money they invested. Reliance Retail, for example, invested $200M and Inc42 reported that it wrote off that investment in January 2025.

Employees

They may lose jobs, and sometimes unpaid wages become claims against the company. Former Dunzo employees reported delayed salaries and other unpaid amounts.

Vendors

Suppliers, landlords, agencies and other businesses may be owed money. Public reporting described unpaid vendor and advertising dues.

Customers

Usually the biggest loss is service: the app disappears, orders stop, warranties/support can become difficult, and prepaid amounts may become claims depending on the situation.

Lenders

Debt holders and other financial creditors may try to recover money from the company's remaining assets according to their legal priority.

Founders

Founders can lose their equity and reputation, but their personal loss is not automatically the same as the company's debt. Personal liability depends on the contracts and law.

Important: A company closing does not mean everyone simply "gets their money back." What each person receives depends on the company's assets, liabilities, contracts, security and the legal process.

In Dunzo's case, this is no longer theoretical: an insolvency process (CIRP) commenced in August 2025, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India was publishing creditor claims in 2026.

So where should we have been checking?

Phase 1 — Prove one small market

Can one city, one customer group and one basic service make healthy money without depending on endless funding?

Phase 2 — Prove the unit

For one order, after delivery, support, discounts, payment costs, wastage and the realistic share of operating costs: are we making money or losing money?

Phase 3 — Prove repeatability

Does the same model work for 10 stores? If not, opening 100 stores does not magically solve it.

Phase 4 — Expand a city

Before entering another city, the existing city should hit agreed targets for contribution, repeat customers, delivery density, wastage, cash cycle and service quality.

Phase 5 — Expand nationally

Only after the economics survive stress tests: lower demand, higher delivery cost, price competition, slower collections and no new funding for 6–12 months.

The micro-level dashboard

This is the part we add to every relevant case. Instead of only asking "Is the company profitable?", ask whether each layer is healthy.

Order

Revenue per order − product cost − delivery − payment/packing/support − discounts = contribution per order.

Store / Hub

Orders × contribution per order − staff − rent − electricity − wastage − local overhead = hub contribution.

City

All hubs + city marketing + city staff + delivery operations − city contribution = city result.

Expansion

New-city setup cost + monthly burn + working capital. Define the maximum loss allowed before stopping.

Cash

Opening cash + collections + funding − salaries − suppliers − rent − taxes − capex − debt = closing cash.

Runway

Cash available ÷ monthly net cash burn. Then stress-test it instead of trusting the base forecast.

Key rule: Revenue growth should never be the only green light. A business can grow revenue while destroying cash.

How do we know whether a new city is ready?

☐ Existing city has positive contribution after real operating costs.
☐ Customer repeat rate is strong enough to avoid buying every order.
☐ Orders are dense enough for efficient routes.
☐ Delivery cost per order is within the target.
☐ Inventory / wastage is controlled.
☐ Supplier payment cycle and customer cash cycle are understood.
☐ New city has a conservative 6–12 month cash plan.
☐ There is a stop rule before expansion begins.

Risks → early warning → action

Orders growing, contribution falling

Warning: every extra order adds less value. Action: stop expansion; fix price, basket size, route density or cost.

Cash burn rising

Warning: runway falls faster than planned. Action: weekly cash review and immediate spending controls.

Funding becomes essential

Warning: business cannot reach next milestone without another round. Action: raise earlier, cut burn, or change the plan — don't wait for zero cash.

One city is weak

Warning: management says "scale will fix it." Action: demand proof before adding more fixed costs.

Competition starts a price war

Warning: customers stay only while subsidised. Action: identify a real advantage and model the business without discounts.

Management ignores warnings

Warning: dashboards exist but decisions don't change. Action: give the board explicit thresholds, owners and escalation rules.

When should a company raise money?

Bad question

"How much can we raise?"

Better question

"What milestone will this money buy, and what proof should exist when we reach it?"

Example

We need ₹10 crore to prove five profitable hubs, not ₹10 crore because "we are growing."

And who owns it?

Finance prepares the numbers. The CEO/founders own the plan. The board challenges and approves major decisions. Investors decide whether to fund based on their rights and agreements.

In a private company with outside investors, shareholder agreements and articles commonly set investor board representation, consent rights and approval rights over matters such as capital structure, borrowing, capex and key management decisions.

Can a system warn us before the company gets into trouble?

Yes — a company can build one. But it cannot guarantee that someone will listen.

Think of it like a smoke alarm. The alarm can detect smoke. It cannot physically stop the owner from ignoring it.

A simple Business Early-Warning System

Daily data→ Weekly dashboard→ Threshold breached→ Automatic alert→ Named owner→ Board escalation

Examples: contribution/order below target for 4 weeks; cash runway below 9 months; vendor dues above threshold; customer repeat falling; city loss above approved limit; expansion spend before milestone approval.

Who should own the warning system?

Finance / CFO

Cash, runway, payables, receivables, budget vs actual, fundraising needs.

Business / Operations

Orders, margin, capacity, delivery cost, quality, wastage, service levels.

Risk / Internal audit

Independent checks: controls, fraud risk, compliance and whether management reporting is reliable.

CEO

Acts on the warning. A dashboard is useless if nobody has authority to change the plan.

Board

Challenges the assumptions, protects the company and makes major capital/strategy decisions.

Investors

Use their contractual and governance rights to monitor and challenge management; they are not a substitute for management.

But what if everyone warns the boss and the boss ignores them?

Question

Then what is the point of the warning system?

Answer

This is the hardest part. A warning system is not just a dashboard. It needs an escalation path that cannot be quietly ignored.

Good governance looks like this:

Red threshold → written alert → named owner → deadline → management response → board visibility → documented decision.

If management chooses to continue despite the warning, the decision and reasoning should be recorded. That creates accountability and lets the board revisit it when conditions change.

Indian private-company governance can include investor nominee directors, reserved matters, board rights and shareholder protections; nominee directors also have duties under the Companies Act.

Could Dunzo's story have been caught earlier?

There were warning signs, yes. We cannot prove that one dashboard would have saved the company.

Public reporting describes losses, aggressive expansion, cash-flow problems, delayed salaries, unpaid vendors, board departures and increasingly difficult fundraising. Those are not subtle signals.

The deeper lesson is more uncomfortable: detecting risk and acting on risk are two different jobs.

If you were starting this business tomorrow...

☐ Know profit/contribution at order level.
☐ Know profit/loss at store or hub level.
☐ Know profit/loss at city level.
☐ Have a stop rule before expanding.
☐ Stress-test sales, prices and costs.
☐ Never make the next funding round part of survival.
☐ Give someone independent authority to raise a red flag.
☐ Decide what happens when the red flag is ignored.

The simple lesson

Don't ask only, "Are we growing?"

Ask: "At what level are we actually making money, where are we losing money, how much cash are we consuming, what could break next, and what will make us stop before the problem becomes too expensive?"

Evidence & limits

Major events and Dunzo's decline are supported by reporting from The Economic Times, Moneycontrol and Rest of World. Dunzo's insolvency process and creditor claims are supported by IBBI/NCLT records. The governance discussion is based on current Indian corporate-governance practice.

Confidence: high for the major reported events and current insolvency status; moderate for the exact contribution of each strategic decision to the eventual failure. Public sources do not provide a complete internal management dashboard, so the proposed warning system is a prevention framework, not a claim that it was Dunzo's actual internal system.
