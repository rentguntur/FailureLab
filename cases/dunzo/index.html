Failure Lab · Case #001

Dunzo

A business that looked like it was winning the race — while the economics underneath were becoming harder to defend.
Startup → scale-up Hyperlocal delivery Quick commerce Growth + unit economics
The short version Running out of money was the final crisis. The more useful lesson is the chain that came before it: aggressive expansion, heavy operating costs, difficult unit economics, dependence on fresh funding, and warning signs that eventually became impossible to ignore.
01 · The story

First, understand the business.

Dunzo began as a hyperlocal service and became one of India's better-known quick-commerce names. By 2022 it had raised a large amount of capital, including a $200 million investment from Reliance Retail, and pushed hard into rapid grocery delivery and expansion.

That is what makes the case interesting. The problem was not that nobody wanted the service. The harder question was whether the business could deliver that service repeatedly at an economic cost that the company could support.

A company can have customers, revenue and a famous investor — and still have a business model that is getting weaker.
02 · Know the business

Before we talk about the failure, meet Dunzo.

If you have never heard of Dunzo, don't worry. Forget the later funding rounds and the quick-commerce race for a moment. Start with the simple idea.

What did Dunzo actually do?

Think of Dunzo as a person you could message and say: “Can you pick this up and bring it to me?”

In its early days, customers could send requests through WhatsApp. Dunzo would help with everyday errands — picking something up, buying something locally, or delivering an item. The basic promise was simple: save me the trip.

Who started it?

Dunzo was founded by Kabeer Biswas, Ankur Aggarwal, Dalvir Suri and Mukund Jha.

The early story is usually dated to 2014, while some company/startup records use 2015. Rather than pretending those dates mean exactly the same thing, Failure Lab keeps the distinction visible.

Where did the idea come from?

The starting problem was ordinary: people spend a surprising amount of time running small errands.

The founders explored a simple question: what if someone else could do those little jobs for you?

So the first version was not a giant delivery network. It was a very hands-on service in Bengaluru.

What did the first Dunzo look like?

It was much smaller and more manual than the company people later knew.

Kabeer initially handled tasks himself. As requests increased, Dunzo brought in part-time help and freelancers. The early service was being worked out in the real world before it became a large technology-and-operations business.

How did it grow from an idea into a company?

1. A small everyday problem “I don't want to spend my time going across the city to get this.”
2. A very simple experiment Customers could message Dunzo through WhatsApp.
3. Humans did the work The founders and early helpers actually completed the errands.
4. People started using it By June 2015, reporting said the team was completing around 70 tasks a day.
5. Outside capital arrived The first reported institutional funding was $650,000 in March 2016.
6. The business became more sophisticated Technology, employees, delivery operations and new business models were added as Dunzo tried to serve a much larger market.

05 · The failure chain

It wasn't one mistake.

The important lesson is the interaction between decisions. A problem at one level made the next decision harder.

1. Growth became the target More cities, stores, delivery capacity and customers meant more infrastructure and spending.
2. The cost base grew with the ambition Dark stores, people, delivery operations, marketing and other operating costs had to be funded continuously.
3. Unit economics remained under pressure In quick commerce, every order has product, fulfilment and delivery costs. Higher volume does not automatically mean higher profit.
4. More capital became necessary When internal cash generation is weak, growth depends on the next funding round.
5. Funding becomes a clock If fundraising takes longer than expected, management has to cut costs, slow expansion or find another source of cash.
6. The cuts arrive after the model has already expanded Layoffs, delayed salaries, unpaid vendor obligations and shrinking operations are not the original problem; they are late-stage signals of financial stress.
7. Trust and strategic options shrink Once investors, employees and vendors lose confidence, raising money or finding a buyer becomes harder.
06 · The business at micro level

Where should a founder check the economics?

Don't jump straight to “Is the company profitable?” That is too late and too broad. Start lower down.

One order

Does the order create positive contribution after the costs directly associated with serving it?

Order revenue − product / fulfilment cost − delivery − incentives / discounts − payment / packing / support = contribution

One store / hub

Can the local fulfilment operation cover its direct operating costs at a realistic order volume?

Store contribution − store people − rent / utilities − local overhead = store result

One city

Does the city work after the costs needed to operate that market? A city can grow orders while still consuming cash.

Expansion

Can the company fund the new stores, people, inventory and customer acquisition required before the new city reaches a stable operating level?

The key discipline: never let company-level growth hide deterioration at order, store or city level.

08 · Expansion gate

Before opening the next city

A useful expansion rule is not “we have money, so let's expand.” The question is whether the existing model has produced enough evidence to justify adding another layer of fixed cost.

The current city has reached an agreed contribution threshold.
Order economics have remained healthy for several months, not just one good week.
The next city has a realistic path to break-even.
The company can fund the expansion without depending on an uncertain funding event.
There is a named person with authority to stop or slow expansion.
A downside scenario has been modelled before the money is committed.
09 · Fundraising

When should a business raise?

Fundraising should buy a specific piece of progress. It should not simply buy more time.

Good question

“What milestone will this money buy, and what proof should exist when we reach it?”

Danger question

“How much money do we need to keep operating until we can raise again?”

For a capital-heavy business, the board should know the answer to three numbers at every meeting: cash today, monthly burn, and realistic runway. Then stress-test what happens if the next round arrives six months late.

10 · Who should act?

A dashboard is useless if nobody owns the warning.

Finance

Own the cash position, runway, burn, payables and financial thresholds.

Operations

Own store, city, delivery and service-level performance.

CEO

Make the cross-company decision when growth, cost and strategy conflict.

Board / investors

Challenge assumptions, review thresholds and escalate when management keeps missing agreed limits.

Important: detection is not action. A dashboard can tell you the business is entering danger. It cannot force a leadership team to stop. That is why every critical metric needs a threshold, an owner, a required action and an escalation path.

10D · Could It Have Been Saved?

Probably — but only before the options became smaller

There is no reliable way to prove a counterfactual. We cannot say that one particular restructuring would definitely have saved Dunzo. But the evidence shows several earlier moments when the business still had more choices.

Window 1 · Before hyper-expansion

Concentrate on the strongest city and micro-markets, prove store economics and expand only from demonstrated performance.

Window 2 · When losses accelerated

Reduce the cash requirement aggressively, protect the core business and make funding a bridge rather than the permanent operating model.

Window 3 · Before stakeholder stress

Delayed salaries, vendor dues and board departures are not just PR problems. They are signals that the recovery window is narrowing.

By FY23, Dunzo's auditor highlighted a material uncertainty around going concern and said continued operations were significantly dependent on additional funding and improved business performance. That is a very late-stage warning. citeturn0search2

10F · Current Status

Where is Dunzo now?

Dunzo Digital entered the corporate insolvency resolution process in August 2025. The IBBI records a final admission order dated 6 August 2025. As of the latest IBBI resolution-plan listing available in 2026, Dunzo Digital was still part of the resolution-plan process, with a Form G published on 6 June 2026 and subsequent EOI deadlines in June/July 2026. citeturn1search1turn0search0

11 · Stakeholders

When the business weakens, who feels it?

Investors

They can lose some or all of the capital they invested. Dunzo's funding history included major institutional investors; Reliance Retail invested $200 million in 2022. Later reporting described the difficulty of securing additional capital and the deterioration of the business.

Employees

Layoffs and delayed salaries can turn a financial problem into an organisational one. Once employees leave, recovery becomes even harder.

Vendors and lenders

Unpaid or delayed obligations become claims against the company and can eventually enter formal legal or insolvency processes.

Customers

The immediate impact is loss or reduction of the service. Any unresolved prepaid amounts or claims have to be treated according to the company's actual legal/insolvency process; customers should not assume automatic full recovery.

12 · Tomorrow Test

If you started this business tomorrow…

Don't copy Dunzo's strategy. Test the assumptions that would make a Dunzo-like business dangerous.

Can one order make a positive contribution without relying on permanent discounts?
Can one store become economically viable at a realistic order volume?
Can one city work before the company opens five more?
What happens if customer growth is 30% slower than planned?
What happens if the next funding round is delayed by 12 months?
How much cash can the company safely burn each month?
Who has authority to freeze expansion?
What exact metric would trigger that freeze?
If management ignores the trigger, who escalates it?
Could the company survive without the next investor?
13 · The lesson

What actually killed the business?

It is too simple to say “Dunzo ran out of money.” That describes the final crisis, not the whole mechanism.

The more useful interpretation is that Dunzo pursued a capital-intensive growth path while its underlying economics and cost structure remained under pressure. Expansion increased the amount of money the business needed. As fundraising became harder, the company had fewer options to absorb the mismatch. The later operational and governance problems were symptoms of a business already under severe financial stress.

Lightbox managing director Sandeep Murthy later said Dunzo lost focus on fundamentals, particularly unit economics and the cost structure of dark stores, and became too focused on capital raising rather than building a sustainable retail model.

The warning to take away isn't “don't grow fast.” It is: don't confuse growth with proof.
