Case #007 · Satyam
Case #007 · Accounting fraud · Governance · Controls

When the numbers stop describing the business.

Satyam was a large, respected Indian IT-services company. On paper, it was growing. Behind the reported numbers, cash, revenue and profits had been fabricated for years.

The useful lesson is not simply “fraud is bad.” It is how a company can make its own reporting system stop being an independent check on reality.
01 · THE BUSINESS

What was Satyam?

Satyam Computer Services sold software development, maintenance, consulting and outsourcing services to global customers. It was a legitimate operating business with thousands of employees and major international clients.

04 · THE WARNING BEFORE THE REVEAL

The Maytas episode was a major governance signal.

December 2008

Satyam proposed acquiring Maytas Properties and Maytas Infra, businesses promoted by members of the founder's family. The proposal triggered strong investor opposition and was withdrawn.

Why it matters

The transaction was not itself proof of the accounting fraud. But it created a governance question: why was a listed IT company proposing a related-party transaction of this scale, and who could independently challenge it?

Failure Lab distinction: Maytas was a warning about governance and capital allocation. The accounting fraud was separately established by subsequent investigations.
05 · THE DISCLOSURE

January 7, 2009 changed the meaning of the balance sheet.

Ramalinga Raju's letter disclosed that the September 30, 2008 balance sheet contained fictitious cash and bank balances of about ₹5,040 crore, understated liabilities of about ₹1,230 crore, and overstated debtors of about ₹490 crore. He also said reported Q2 revenue of ₹2,700 crore should have been about ₹2,112 crore, while reported operating margin of ₹649 crore should have been about ₹61 crore. citeturn1search2

06 · WHAT WAS ACTUALLY HAPPENING

The gap had become a system.

Reported

Revenue and operating profit were presented as steadily strong.

Underlying

The company had a real IT-services business, but reported figures included fictitious revenue, cash, interest and receivables.

Control failure

Multiple accounting systems and weak independent verification allowed false entries to survive across periods.

Satyam's later forensic investigation described a complex reporting environment with multiple non-integrated financial systems. It found fictitious entries and unrecorded transactions affecting revenue, cash, bank balances, receivables and other accounts. citeturn1search62

07 · THE 10-MINUTE FAILURE SCAN

What should an owner, board or investor check?

EconomicsDo reported margins reconcile with delivery capacity and real costs?
CashCan reported cash be independently confirmed at the bank?
ReceivablesDo large customer balances reconcile to external confirmations and collections?
ObligationsDo liabilities, debt and related-party movements reconcile?
OperationsDo headcount, projects and customers support the reported revenue?
ManagementAre executives making unusual transactions that protect the reported story?
ControlsCan anyone senior to the operating team independently challenge the numbers?
DecisionWhat happens if one critical reported balance cannot be verified?
Control principle: the most important numbers should not depend entirely on the same people who benefit from those numbers looking good.
08 · FAILURE CHAIN

How the reporting problem became a corporate crisis.

Pressure to maintain reported growth and profitability
↓
Fictitious revenue and income entries
↓
Fictitious cash and receivables make the balance sheet support the story
↓
The gap grows as real performance cannot catch up
↓
Large strategic transactions increase the need to preserve credibility
↓
Independent checks fail to break the loop
↓
Disclosure exposes a large mismatch between reported and economic reality

Root-cause discipline: the fraud itself was deliberate misconduct by individuals. The organisational lesson is broader: governance and verification systems failed to stop, detect or surface the manipulation earlier.

11 · DECISION GATES

Build controls around the numbers that can kill the business.

Cash gate

Rule: independently verify material cash balances every reporting period.

Fail: unexplained mismatch → no sign-off until resolved.

Revenue gate

Rule: reconcile major revenue streams to contracts, delivery evidence and collections.

Fail: unexplained revenue → investigate before reporting.

Related-party gate

Rule: material related-party transactions require genuinely independent review.

Fail: conflict or unclear value → stop the transaction.

Board-information gate

Rule: directors receive raw evidence, not only management-selected summaries.

Fail: restricted access → treat the control environment as impaired.

12 · COULD IT HAVE BEEN STOPPED?

The counterfactual is about controls, not certainty.

This is Failure Lab analysis, not a claim that one control would definitely have prevented the fraud.

Before the gap became huge

Independent cash confirmations and customer receivable confirmations could have tested whether reported assets existed.

Before Maytas

A truly independent board process could have treated the related-party proposal as a governance stress test rather than only a transaction.

Before disclosure

Segregated financial systems, direct audit access and stronger challenge rights could have increased the probability of earlier detection.

13 · WHO SHOULD HAVE ACTED?

Controls fail when responsibility becomes everyone’s job and nobody’s job.

Management

Provide truthful records and allow independent verification.

Board

Challenge unusual transactions and demand evidence independent of management.

Auditors

Test existence and completeness of material balances rather than relying excessively on representations.

Audit committee

Escalate unexplained inconsistencies and conflicts.

Regulators

Investigate credible market and disclosure anomalies and enforce consequences.

Investors

Ask whether reported cash, margins and growth are supported by independently observable evidence.

14 · CURRENT STATUS

Satyam survived as an operating business, but not as an independent company.

The post-scandal company was restructured and became Mahindra Satyam. It was later merged with Tech Mahindra in 2013. So the correct lesson is not “Satyam disappeared overnight.” The operating business was rescued and absorbed, while the original governance and corporate identity did not survive.

Why this matters: crisis resolution and business survival are different questions. A company can survive operationally while the original institution, leadership and ownership structure are dismantled.
15 · FINAL FAILURE TREE

One failure pattern, compressed.

Primary misconduct: financial statements were deliberately falsified.
↓
Enabling conditions: weak independent verification, information asymmetry and governance conflicts.
↓
Reinforcing mechanism: each period's reported success increased the pressure to preserve the story.
↓
Terminal event: disclosure destroyed confidence in the reported numbers.
↓
Outcome: government intervention, restructuring, legal/regulatory action and eventual merger.
16 · TOMORROW TEST

If you were starting this business tomorrow, what would you check so you don't repeat this mistake?

1. Can the number be independently verified?

For every material cash, receivable and revenue balance, identify the evidence outside the person who prepared the number.

2. Who can say “no”?

Name the independent person or committee with authority to stop a transaction or reject management's numbers.

3. What happens when evidence disagrees?

Write the escalation rule before a crisis: mismatch → investigation → freeze sign-off → board escalation.

4. Does growth survive a reality check?

Compare reported revenue and margin with collections, customer confirmations, delivery evidence, staffing and cash movement.

The practical lesson: don't ask only “Do the numbers look good?” Ask “What independent evidence would prove these numbers are real?”
