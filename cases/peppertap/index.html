Case #002 · Startup · Grocery delivery

PepperTap

It grew incredibly fast. It raised more than $50 million. It reached roughly 20,000 orders a day at its peak. Then, within months, it shut its consumer grocery business. The interesting question is not just why it failed. It is: why did the business keep losing money as it scaled?

Founded 2014Gurgaon, IndiaConsumer grocery business shut Apr 2016~$51M reported funding
01 · Understand the business

First, what was PepperTap?

What did it do?

PepperTap was an app-based grocery delivery service. Customers ordered groceries and household items, and PepperTap arranged the picking and delivery from partner stores.

Who started it?

Navneet Singh and Milind Sharma founded PepperTap in 2014. Both had prior experience in logistics and e-commerce, including work connected to Delhivery and their earlier logistics venture, Nuvo Ex.

What was clever about the model?

It was largely inventory-less. Instead of owning a large grocery warehouse network, PepperTap connected customers to existing local stores and handled the technology and delivery layer.

What was the promise?

Convenience: order groceries from your phone and get them delivered quickly, with PepperTap initially promising delivery within about two hours.

The hidden challenge: PepperTap avoided the cost of owning all the inventory and warehouses, but that did not make the delivery economics easy. It still had to acquire customers, integrate with stores, maintain delivery capacity and fulfil the promised service even when demand was uneven.

06 · The failure mechanism
07 · FINANCIAL SNAPSHOT

The growth number was not the health number

There are different reported peak-order figures, so Failure Lab should not pretend there is one perfectly settled number. Navneet Singh's April 2016 post-mortem said PepperTap was doing about 20,000 orders a day by October 2015 and later referred to 37,000 orders a day in its peak period. Other contemporaneous reports cited lower figures. What is much more important—and better supported—is the direction: order volume had been very high, then collapsed as the company pulled back. The founder also said the business was losing money on every order.

Evidence discipline: We use the 20,000 and 37,000 figures as founder/company claims, not as independently reconciled operating statistics. The core conclusion does not depend on choosing between them.

Do not confuse these numbers: a high order count proves demand and activity. It does not prove that the business is becoming economically healthier.

It wasn't one mistake.

PepperTap's own post-mortem, reported at the time, pointed to three major problems: deep discounting, scaling faster than its operating processes could support, and the high cost of maintaining supply. It also faced difficult store-integration and inventory-data problems.

08 · The money test
08 · THE MONEY JOURNEY

Where the money went

PepperTap had raised substantial outside capital and used it to build a fast-growing consumer delivery network. The problem was not simply that it spent money. The deeper problem was that the core order economics were still negative, so growth kept creating a larger cash requirement.

Capital raisedFunding gave the company room to build and expand.
→
Growth spendingDiscounts, delivery capacity, technology and city expansion.
→
Negative contributionEach delivery still consumed cash after the relevant costs.
→
Higher cash needMore orders and cities required more operating cash.
→
Funding pressureWhen fresh capital became harder to secure, the model had less room to continue.
Failure Lab lesson: Funding can buy time. It cannot permanently repair negative economics.

The question should have been: does one more order help?

Money coming in

Customer payment + any delivery fee + commission/revenue from the transaction.

Money going out

Delivery cost + discounts + customer acquisition + operational overhead + technology/integration cost.

The key warning: If the company loses money on each order, then “more orders” can make the loss larger. Scale only helps when the economics improve with scale.

09 · CASE-SPECIFIC ECONOMICS TRAP

The PepperTap trap: more orders could mean more cash leaving

This is the mechanism worth remembering. PepperTap was not struggling because customers refused to use the service. The problem was that each additional order could still carry discounts, delivery capacity and operating costs that were larger than the economic value created by the order.

Low-margin groceriesLittle room for error.
→
Need customersGrowth depended heavily on acquisition.
→
Deep discountsCustomers were subsidised to try and return.
→
Ready-to-deliver capacityTwo-hour delivery required capacity even when demand was uneven.
→
Negative contributionMore volume did not reliably make each order profitable.
↓
More volume → larger cash requirement → greater dependence on new capital
Failure Lab check: Before scaling, prove that the next order improves contribution after the costs that actually move with that order. Do not let “orders are growing” substitute for that test.
10 · Warning signals
11 · GROWTH TEST

Why more orders did not fix the problem

This is the key question to carry into any delivery business: when we add one more order, does the business become stronger? In PepperTap's case, the answer was not reliably yes because discounts and delivery capacity kept the economics under pressure.

More customersDemand rises.
→
More ordersVolume rises.
→
More delivery costCapacity and service obligations rise too.
→
More discountsAcquisition and retention still need support.
→
More cash burnIf contribution stays negative, scale multiplies the problem.

Healthy scaling

More volume improves density, lowers cost per order, strengthens repeat demand and moves contribution toward positive.

Dangerous scaling

More volume requires more subsidies, more capacity and more cash while contribution remains negative.

14 · Counterfactual

What could management have done differently?

These are analysis, not claims about what would definitely have saved PepperTap.

Window 1 — before mass expansion

Prove that a grocery order could approach positive contribution without depending on extreme discounts. If not, do not multiply the model across cities.

Window 2 — after early city failures

Turn city closures into a hard expansion rule: no new city until existing markets hit predefined economics and service-quality thresholds.

Window 3 — when funding sentiment changed

Model the business assuming no new funding. That would force the real question: how quickly can the company become cash-generative, and what must be cut if it cannot?

Window 4 — final decision

The pivot to logistics shows management did eventually stop the grocery model. The lesson is to make that decision while the company still has strategic options—not only when the market has already decided.

15 · Could it have been saved?

Maybe the business could have been made smaller. That is different from proving the original model would work.

The strongest evidence suggests the problem was structural enough that simply adding more customers was not the answer. The founder said the company was losing money on each delivery and saw profitability as distant.

Most plausible rescue: stop broad expansion, sharply reduce subsidy, concentrate on a few dense micro-markets, fix store integration, and require each retained market to prove improving contribution. Even then, success would not be guaranteed.
16 · Stakeholders

18 · Tomorrow Test

If you started this business tomorrow…

Before spending heavily on growth, I would want answers to these questions:

  1. What is our contribution per order after every directly attributable cost?
  2. How much of demand disappears when discounts are removed?
  3. How many orders does one delivery resource need to cover its cost?
  4. How accurate is store inventory data?
  5. What percentage of orders are cancelled, substituted or delayed because the store data is wrong?
  6. Does each city become economically healthier as it gets denser?
  7. If we receive zero new funding for 18 months, what changes immediately?
  8. What exact threshold makes us stop expanding a city?
19 · The lesson

Growth can hide a broken engine.

PepperTap is useful because the failure was not a story of “nobody wanted the product.” People clearly did use it. The harder lesson is that a customer can love a service while the business loses money serving that customer.

The early-warning question is therefore not “Are orders growing?” It is:

“When we add one more customer, one more order, one more store and one more city—does the business become stronger, or does it simply become bigger?”
