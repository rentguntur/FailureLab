FAILURE LAB · CASE #010

Bira 91: When a compliance change became an operating crisis

Bira 91 did not lose its market because customers suddenly stopped wanting the beer. A legal-name change ahead of a planned IPO interacted badly with India’s state-by-state alcohol licensing system. The result was a long supply interruption, inventory write-offs and a cash squeeze that then exposed deeper financial and governance weaknesses.

2015–2026Regulatory dependency · change management · working capitalConsumer brand in a highly regulated market

01 · The business

Bira 91 built a premium craft-beer brand in a market dominated by large brewers. It grew through distinctive branding, new beer variants, breweries and a widening distribution footprint.

Customer
Consumers buying beer through licensed retail, restaurants and bars.
Promise
A modern Indian craft-beer brand with differentiated taste, packaging and positioning.
Hard constraint
In India, legal permission to make, label, distribute and sell alcohol depends heavily on state-level excise rules and approvals.

That third item is not background paperwork. For this business, regulatory continuity is part of the operating system.

Failure point: the company treated a corporate-identity change as if it were mainly an administrative event. In a state-regulated alcohol market, it could interrupt the legal ability to sell the product itself.

04 · Why it looked like a small change

B9 Beverages changed its legal name from B9 Beverages Private Ltd to B9 Beverages Ltd as part of preparations for a potential public listing. The change was one word on the corporate name. The operating consequences were much larger because state excise systems had to recognise the new legal entity and product registrations.

The important Failure Lab lesson is not “never change your company name.” It is: before changing an identity that appears on regulated licences, map every downstream dependency.

05 · The first serious warning

By 2023–24, the name change had triggered fresh label registrations, approvals and licensing work across states. Founder Ankur Jain later said the process created a roughly four-to-six-month period in which the company could not sell normally despite demand. Economic Times reported that about ₹80 crore of inventory had to be written off because the old labels could not be used under the changed legal identity.

Early-warning interpretation: if a compliance project can stop revenue for months, it is not a back-office project. It is a business-continuity project and should be run with a launch-readiness gate.

06 · What the evidence later showed

FY24 financial statements show how a regulatory interruption can travel through a consumer business. B9 Beverages reported a ₹6,449.70 million net loss for the year ended March 31, 2024 and negative operating cash flow of ₹422.63 million. The audit report said accumulated losses had reached ₹21,179.75 million and current liabilities exceeded current assets by ₹4,876.63 million. The auditors said these conditions created material uncertainty about going concern, while management relied on expected growth, funding and liquidity measures.

Contemporaneous reporting put the FY24 revenue at about ₹638 crore, down from roughly ₹824 crore in FY23, with net loss around ₹748 crore. Sales volumes fell from about 9 million cases to 6–7 million cases. The exact figures differ slightly across reporting boundaries, so the robust lesson is the direction: revenue and volume fell sharply while losses and liquidity pressure worsened.

The name change was therefore a major trigger, but it should not be treated as the sole explanation for the later crisis. Reporting also points to pre-existing cash pressure, expansion spending, underutilised capacity, fundraising difficulty and governance conflict. The case is about how one preventable operational disruption can expose a fragile financial system.

07 · 10-Minute Failure Scan

Economics
Check whether each market and brewery is contributing cash after trade spend, logistics and regulatory costs.
Cash
Model a zero-sales period of 30, 60, 90 and 120 days in a key state before making a regulatory change.
Balance sheet
Map vendor, employee, tax, lender and lease obligations against unrestricted cash.
Operations
List every licence, label, product registration, distributor agreement and warehouse dependency touched by a legal-entity change.
Growth
Do not add breweries, brands or acquisitions while the existing network cannot absorb a regulatory interruption.
Management information
Ask which single external approval could legally stop revenue tomorrow — and whether that dependency has an owner and contingency plan.

Reinforcing loop: lost sales → lower cash → overdue payments → weaker supplier confidence → harder operations → even less cash. A compliance disruption becomes a financing problem when the business has too little liquidity buffer.

11 · Decision Gates

Gate 1 — Before a legal-entity change
Do a state-by-state licence and label impact map. No filing until every critical dependency has an owner.
Gate 2 — Before the old licence is surrendered
Obtain written confirmation that the new entity can legally sell every active SKU in each major market.
Gate 3 — Before building inventory
Do not manufacture months of stock whose labels or approvals depend on an unresolved regulatory transition.
Gate 4 — Before expansion
Maintain enough liquidity to survive the longest plausible regulatory interruption without missing payroll, tax or critical suppliers.

12 · Could it have been stopped?

The specific regulatory shock was probably preventable. The company could have treated the legal-name change as a regulated go-live project rather than a corporate filing.

A stronger counterfactual would have been: keep the existing licensed identity where legally possible, obtain approvals in advance, run a state-by-state cutover plan, maintain dual packaging inventory only where permitted, and hold a dedicated cash buffer for the transition.

But that would not automatically have solved every later problem. The audited accounts and later reporting show that the business also had deeper losses, liquidity pressure and governance challenges.

14 · Current status

Bira 91 is not accurately described as simply “gone.” In July 2026, Economic Times reported that founder Ankur Jain stepped down from the board and gave up executive control and ownership under a settlement with investors and lenders. The agreement is intended to recapitalise the company and restart operations after production had halted.

A separate July 2026 report said Anicut Capital had taken over Jain’s stake and would lead restructuring, with the company facing roughly ₹1,000 crore of debt and liabilities. The current story is therefore a turnaround / recapitalisation attempt after severe operational and financial distress, not a completed liquidation.

15 · Final Failure Tree

Root conditions
Highly regulated distribution + rapid growth + thin tolerance for long revenue interruptions.
Trigger
Legal-entity change required fresh approvals, labels and registrations across states.
Reinforcing mechanism
Sales interruption → inventory write-off → working-capital stress → overdue obligations → funding and governance pressure.
Pattern added to the Failure Lab library: regulatory change can be an operating event, not a compliance event. If a licence controls whether customers can legally buy the product, compliance belongs inside the revenue model.
