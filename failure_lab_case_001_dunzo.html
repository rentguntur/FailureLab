Case #001 · India · Delivery / Quick Commerce

They had customers. They had big investors. So what happened to Dunzo?

Dunzo started as a clever, useful service: you needed something picked up or dropped off, and Dunzo handled it. Later, it moved into a much harder game — trying to deliver groceries very quickly from its own stores. This is the story of how that change, rapid expansion, heavy spending, competition and a shrinking funding window came together.

Okay, let's start simple.

Friend A

Was Dunzo a bad idea from the beginning?

Friend B

No. That's actually what makes this story interesting.

Friend B

Dunzo had a product people understood. It became well known for getting things from one place to another, especially in Bengaluru.

Friend A

So where did it go wrong?

The important change came when Dunzo moved much harder into quick commerce: very fast grocery delivery using its own network of neighbourhood warehouses, often called dark stores. That put it into a fight with much deeper-pocketed competitors such as Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart.

The story in one line

Dunzo did not simply “run out of money.”
It moved into a capital-hungry business, expanded aggressively, struggled to make the economics work at scale, faced stronger competition, and then found it much harder to raise the next round when the funding environment changed.

That distinction matters. “Ran out of money” describes the final fire. We want to understand what was feeding it.

Let's go back to the beginning.

2014
Dunzo starts as a hyperlocal service.

The original idea was closer to a digital helper: pick up a parcel, buy something, deliver something, connect local demand with local stores.

2020–2021
Quick commerce takes off.

Dunzo launches Dunzo Daily and starts moving toward fast grocery delivery from its own stores.

January 2022
Reliance Retail invests $200M.

Dunzo now has the money and investor backing to push much harder into growth.

2022
Expansion gets expensive.

Dunzo expands across more cities and stores and spends heavily on marketing. Reporting later put one IPL campaign at about ₹40 crore.

2023
The warning signs become hard to ignore.

Fundraising gets harder. Layoffs begin. Salary payments are delayed. The company cuts back stores and tries to reduce costs while still searching for more funding.

2024
Survival mode.

Investors pull back from board participation, the company keeps shrinking, and attempts to raise more money or find a transaction struggle.

January 2025
The platform goes offline.

After founder Kabeer Biswas leaves for Flipkart, Dunzo's app and website go offline and the business is effectively no longer operating as the service people knew.

Wait. Was growth itself the problem?

Friend A

They raised $200 million. Why not just use that money to grow?

Friend B

Because growth isn't automatically good. If every new store and delivery order costs more than it earns, growing faster can make the hole bigger.

Friend B

Quick commerce needs inventory nearby, warehouses, delivery workers, technology, discounts and lots of orders. All of that costs money before you know whether the economics work.

Friend A

So the real question isn't “How fast are sales growing?” It's “Does each extra order make the business healthier?”

One big warning sign: the model was getting harder, not easier.

Dunzo was trying to compete in a market where speed, assortment, availability and price all mattered. Contemporary reporting said the company struggled to make its quick-commerce economics work, while rivals were scaling aggressively. Later, Lightbox's Sandeep Murthy said Dunzo had lost focus on business fundamentals, particularly unit economics and the cost of dark stores.

Plain-English warning sign: If a business needs a lot of cash just to keep delivering the product, prove very carefully that the business can eventually stand on its own.

Then the competition got serious.

Dunzo wasn't competing in a quiet market. Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart were all pushing hard into quick commerce. Rest of World reported that Dunzo expanded to 15 cities and around 120 dark stores, while competitors were building much larger store networks. Former employees described operational problems around stores, fulfilment and rider support as the company expanded.

More cities→More stores→More fixed costs→Need more orders→Need more cash

Expansion is not just “opening another location.” Every new location creates another set of costs, people, inventory, mistakes and things that can go wrong.

Why didn't they just stop earlier?

Friend A

If the economics weren't working, why keep going?

Friend B

Because stopping a big bet is painful. You already have employees, stores, investors, customers and a huge amount of money invested.

Friend B

And there was still a possibility that more scale would improve the numbers.

Friend A

But then they needed more money to reach that scale.

Friend B

Right. That's the trap: “We need more money to prove the model, but investors want the model to look stronger before they give us more money.”

The failure chain

This is our interpretation of the sequence, based on the reporting used for this case. It is not a claim that one decision alone caused the collapse.

Move into quick commerce→Aggressive expansion→High ongoing costs→Economics remain difficult→Competition intensifies→Funding gets harder→Cuts + delayed payments→Loss of operating stability

Could we have spotted it earlier?

Probably yes — but not with certainty.

By 2022–23, several things were visible: the business was spending heavily to expand, quick-commerce competition was intensifying, the company was trying to change its model, fundraising was becoming difficult, and later it began cutting jobs and delaying salaries. Those are serious warning signs.

What we cannot honestly say is that the eventual shutdown was obvious from the first day of the pivot. The market was changing quickly, and competitors themselves were also investing heavily. The better lesson is: prove the economics and your right to win before making the cost base enormous.

If you were starting this business tomorrow...

Before opening the next store, city or delivery hub, I would want answers to these questions:

☐ Does one order make money after real delivery and operating costs?
☐ Does that improve as order density increases?
☐ What happens if orders are 30–50% below plan?
☐ How much cash does one new city consume before it becomes healthy?
☐ Can the existing city work well before adding five more?
☐ Why will customers choose us when a bigger rival cuts prices?
☐ What is our real advantage — not just “we were first”?
☐ If funding stops tomorrow, can the business survive?
☐ What number would make us stop expanding?
☐ Are we growing because customers want more, or because investors expect growth?

The simple lesson

Don't confuse growth with proof.

More customers, more cities, more stores and more funding can make a company look bigger. They don't automatically make the underlying business healthier. Before scaling, prove that the basic engine works.

What we know — and what we don't

Well-supported: Dunzo started in 2014; Reliance Retail invested $200M in 2022; the company expanded its quick-commerce operations; it later faced fundraising difficulty, layoffs, delayed salaries and operational problems; and its app/website went offline in January 2025.

Our analysis: the failure is best understood as a chain involving a difficult capital-intensive model, aggressive expansion, competition, weak or unproven economics at the required scale, and then a funding environment that became less forgiving.

Not claimed: that any single person or single decision “caused” the failure. The public evidence does not justify such a simple verdict.

Confidence: High on the major timeline and observable events; moderate on the exact weighting of individual root causes.
